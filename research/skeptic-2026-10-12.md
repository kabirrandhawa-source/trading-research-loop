## What the engine actually measures (applies to all three)

- **Fills.** A decision on bar t's close fills at the open of t+1 (delay=1). An exit decided on the
  close of the fill bar fills at the next open. So a "1-day hold" is **open(t+1) to open(t+2)**, never
  close to close. Under the delay=2 stress the whole window moves one more bar later.
- **Costs.** 5 bp one-way, so 10 bp a round trip. The 2x stress makes that 20 bp a round trip.
- **Walk-forward.** The dev set (2016 to Jan 2024) is cut into 7 equal chunks, and the first chunk is
  never traded out of sample. The OOS period is therefore roughly mid-2017 to Jan 2024, about 6.7
  years in 5 folds of about 16 months each.
- **Default sizing and stops.** `max_position_pct = 10` and `stop_loss_pct = 5` apply unless the
  strategy overrides them. Every memo assumes 100% notional and no stop. Sharpe is mostly unaffected
  by scale, but the 5% stop is an *undeclared extra parameter*. It could cut an XLF window trade
  early. Each strategy file should set these to match its memo, or the memo is not what gets tested.
- **The stress tests are reported but do not gate anything.** A failed delay=2 run will not block
  promotion on its own, so the reader has to act on it.
- **Correction to the macro memo.** The dev data runs to Jan 2024, so **2022 sits inside the dev
  walk-forward**, not inside the sealed 25% as the memo says. The hiking-cycle stress test happens in
  development, and the sealed period is 2024 onward.

## Idea 3 — QQQ low-IBS one-day mean reversion (price_analyst)

**The strongest case that it fails**

1. **The engine destroys the mechanism.** The claim is close-to-close reversal after a close near the
   day's low. The engine buys at the *next open*. With short-horizon reversal in index ETFs, a large
   share of the bounce usually shows up as an overnight gap, and buying at the next open forfeits
   that gap. What is left is open(t+1) to open(t+2). That window mostly holds the generic overnight
   equity drift from t+1 to t+2, which buy-and-hold also earns. The in-sample figures (0.32% vs 0.08%
   next-day mean, Sharpe 0.92) are all close-to-close, so **none of them carry over to this engine**.
   Damage: **severe**. The delay=2 stress (open t+2 to open t+3) acts on a two-day-stale signal and
   should come out close to zero.
2. **No risk-adjusted edge even in sample.** The memo's own numbers give a Sharpe of 0.92 against 0.90
   for buy-and-hold QQQ. The best in-sample case adds about 0.02 of Sharpe and takes on about 74 fills a
   year of execution risk to get it.
3. **Costs.** About 37 round trips a year means 3.7%/yr of drag at 10 bp and 7.4%/yr at 2x. The memo
   already shows Sharpe falling from 0.92 to 0.59 at 10 bp one-way. The engine's 2x-cost stress is
   that same 10 bp one-way, and it applies on top of the open-fill damage.
4. **Data-snooping.** About 40 variants were tried across 15 tickers. "I chose QQQ, not the top scorer
   XLK" gives almost no protection, because QQQ and XLK are highly correlated tech baskets. The rule
   failed on SPY (0.07), DIA (0.08) and IWM (0.23) and was negative on IEF, GLD and XLI. The most
   likely reading is that this is "long tech dips during 2016–2023", which is a beta and regime bet
   rather than a microstructure effect. The sweep peak sits at 0.30, the edge of the range tested,
   not at 0.20.
5. **Crowding and decay.** IBS and RSI(2) dip-buying on index ETFs has been published widely in
   practitioner work for over a decade. Publicly known edges like this tend to decay.
6. **Regime dependence.** Sharpe was about 0 in 2019 and negative in 2022 and in the 2024 stub. It
   works when tech goes up.
7. **Implementation trap.** "Consecutive signals keep the position open" needs `exit()` to recheck IBS.
   Otherwise the engine churns: it exits, then misses the bar while the order is pending, then
   re-enters. That adds costs that do not exist in the in-sample numbers.

Survivorship and look-ahead are not concerns. The ETF exists for the whole period, and the rule uses
only bar t.

**Verdict: DROP.** The engine cannot test the hypothesis as stated, the in-sample edge over buy-and-hold
is about zero, and the selection process was heavy. If it is ever revived, it needs an engine with
market-on-close fills. Running it here would only measure overnight drift.

## Idea 2 — Pre-FOMC drift in SPY (macro_watcher)

**The strongest case that it fails**

1. **Post-publication decay.** Lucca & Moench used data from 1994 to 2011. Later work, which the memo
   cites itself, reports that the drift weakened sharply after publication. The 2016–2023 sample is
   entirely post-publication, so a null result is the base case.
2. **Window mismatch, made worse by the engine.** The effect runs from 2pm T-1 to 2pm T. The memo's
   daily approximation is close T-1 to close T. The engine turns that into **open T to open T+1**:
   - It loses the T-1 afternoon and the overnight into T, which is a real part of the original drift.
   - It keeps the morning of T, the 2pm statement, the press-conference reaction *and* the overnight
     after the event.
   Damage: **moderate to large**. Most of what gets measured is the announcement reaction, which has no
   documented sign, plus overnight drift. The pre-declared variant (decide on close T-2, exit on close
   T) becomes open T-1 to open T+1. That variant covers the whole pre-announcement window but still
   includes the reaction.
3. **Delay=2 is a placebo, not a stress test.** The dates are scheduled in advance, so a real trader
   would never be "a day late". Delay=2 moves the window to open T+1 to open T+2, which is entirely
   after the event and should come out null. It should be read as a placebo. A positive placebo would
   be a red flag that the result is generic drift.
4. **Too few trades.** About 52 events fall in the walk-forward OOS, or about 10 per fold. One
   statement-day crash or rally can decide a fold. The memo's "drop the best 2 events" check is
   necessary.
5. **Regime dependence.** The proposed mechanism (an accommodative Fed and risk-premium release)
   depends on the regime. 2022–23 is in dev and is the honest test.
6. **Small hindsight choices.** Excluding March 2020 is defensible but was decided after the fact. Dates
   are hard-coded. They match the public schedule (I checked the list, and 2024-01-31 falls after the
   dev end of 2024-01-29, so it is correctly excluded). Scheduled dates are known in advance, so there
   is no look-ahead.

Costs are negligible: about 8 round trips a year, or about 0.8%/yr of drag at full notional and 10 bp,
against a claimed per-event edge of tens of bp. Crowding is plausible because the effect is famous,
which also explains the decay. Survivorship does not apply.

**Why it still deserves a test.** It has zero free parameters, a published prior with a known effect
size, and an event schedule that is fixed in advance. With about 52 events and a per-day SPY standard
deviation of about 1.1–1.3%, the standard error of the mean is about 0.15–0.18%. That is enough
**power to reject the original effect size** (around 0.3–0.5% per event) if it is gone. A clean
negative is useful information for the ledger.

**Verdict: TEST**, with these conditions: pre-register the engine window (primary: decide on close
T-1, giving open T to open T+1; variant: decide on close T-2 and hold through T, giving open T-1 to
open T+1); set `max_position_pct` and `stop_loss_pct` to match the memo; treat delay=2 as a placebo;
and judge against the mean open-to-open return of a random SPY day, not against zero.

## Idea 1 — XLF big-bank earnings-window premium (fundamentals_reader)

**The strongest case that it fails**

1. **No statistical power.** There are at most 4 windows a year. The 100d SMA filter removes some, so
   about 18–22 trades land in the walk-forward OOS, or about 4 per fold. A 9–10 trading-day XLF window
   has a standard deviation of roughly 4–5%, so the standard error of the mean is about 1%. The
   earnings-announcement premium is a *single-stock, idiosyncratic-risk* effect of tens of bp. At ETF
   level only part of the weight reports inside the window (BRK.B and most insurers do not), and the
   idiosyncratic part diversifies away. A realistic signal is perhaps 0.2–0.4% per window against
   about 1% of noise. **The test cannot tell this effect apart from zero in either direction.** A
   "pass" would most likely be luck.
2. **Mechanism mismatch.** The literature explains the premium as compensation for firm-specific
   announcement risk and as attention-driven buying of individual names. Neither clearly survives
   aggregation into a sector ETF.
3. **Snooping through the narrative.** The 100-day SMA is justified by naming the episodes it would
   have avoided (COVID in 2020, the March 2023 bank failures). Those are in-sample events chosen with
   hindsight. The memo's own test, filter versus no filter, is the right guard.
4. **Variant grid.** Entry days 5–9 × exit days 18–22 gives 25 variants on about 20 trades. That is
   plenty of room to find a positive cell by chance. Only the base case should count.
5. **Regime and composition.** Real estate left XLF in Sept 2016, and bank-heavy weights moved with the
   rate cycle. Window returns in 2016–2023 depend on rate-path news (NII in 2022–23) more than on any
   attention premium.

Engine damage is **small**. Moving close-to-close to open-to-open shifts a roughly 9-day window by one
overnight, and delay=2 shifts it one more day. Costs are trivial at about 4 round trips a year. Watch
the default 5% stop, which could fire inside a 2-week window and change the trade.

**Verdict: DROP** (low priority rather than wrong). The engine can run it faithfully, but the sample
cannot answer the question, so any result is uninformative and still uses a variation slot. A better
use is to revisit it with an actual earnings-date feed and single-stock data, or pool it across many
sectors and seasons to get the trade count into the hundreds.
